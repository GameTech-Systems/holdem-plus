Hold’em Plus Poker Strategy Guide

Texas Hold’em with one extra hole card, dealt after the flop. Everything else still applies, but best-5-of-8 changes which hands are worth playing and how strong a “good” hand really is.

What the extra card does: it pulls every hand toward average. Heads-up, AA falls from 85% to 75% while 72o rises from 35% to 40%. Against a full table, premium pairs lose the most (AA 49% to 35% against five hands). The gain follows weakness more than shape: the biggest gainers are the weakest hands (54s +7.7 points heads-up, 52o +7.3), and a suited connector gains only about 0.3 points more than the same connector offsuit. Big offsuit hands lose (A9o: 61% to 54%).

Your plus card arrives before flop betting, so you decide with all 3 cards and the flop in view. Odds are the chance the plus card is one of your outs. Averaged over all flops that is outs ÷ 50. Once you have seen the flop, count the outs still unseen and divide by 47: every out the flop already used lowers it. Here is what the plus card did to your hand:

Core principles

  • The flop is the real decision street. It is the first betting round where every player has all 3 cards.
  • Think nuts, not “good hand.” With 8 cards to choose from, second-best flushes and straights lose more often than they do in Hold’em.
  • One pair is weaker. Top pair with a weak kicker is a bluff-catcher; fold it to large river bets more than you would in Hold’em.
  • Charge the draws. More outs and combo draws mean bigger bets on wet boards, and more semi-bluffs when you hold the draw.
  • Watch the board. Paired boards make full houses and quads more likely, and a flush or straight can be beaten by a hand you did not consider.
  • Pot odds are unchanged. The rule of 2 and 4 still estimates your equity; count the extra outs your 3rd card gives, but discount them. Some outs also improve an opponent, overlapping draws share cards, and with three hole cards you will often hold several draws at once.
  • Rabbit Runner is information, not value. It cannot change the hand you folded. Treat it as a paid extra for curiosity or learning. In tournaments the fee is cash or e-notes on DAS, never tournament chips.

Ring game vs tournament

Ring game (cash)

  • Every chip is worth its face value, so aim to maximize chip EV: take +EV risks, including all-ins, within what your bankroll can stand.
  • Deep stacks reward implied odds: suited aces, suited connectors and small pairs gain value. The usual set-mining rule (stacks of about 15–20 times the raise) comes from Hold’em, where a pocket pair flops a set 1 time in 8.5. Here a pocket pair is a set or better by flop betting 1 time in 6.4 (15.5%), which lowers the stack you need, but sets also lose to flushes and straights more often. Treat 15–20× as a starting point, not a tested figure.
  • Position is your main edge. Open wider on the button and cutoff, much tighter from early seats.
  • Thin value bets with one pair lose value, since opponents’ calling ranges beat you more often.
  • Pick tables with loose, passive, multiway pots. Your strong hands get paid.
  • Rebuy when short if the game and your bankroll allow it; there is no survival value to protect.

Tournament

  • Chips lost cost more than chips won gain (ICM). Avoid marginal all-ins near the bubble and pay jumps; chip leaders should apply pressure.
  • Depth zones: above 30 big blinds, play normally. 15–30: drop speculative hands, raise or fold. 10–15: shove or fold. Under 10: push or fold only. These cut-offs are standard Hold’em heuristics and have not been tested for Hold’em Plus.
  • Blinds rise on a clock (the demo starts at 1/2 with 40 chips and 15-minute levels), so stacks shrink fast. Steal blinds early.
  • Heads-up, the button is the small blind and acts first preflop. Play wide: any ace, any pair, suited and broadway cards.
  • Premium edges shrink: against a random hand AA is a 75% favorite heads-up here, against 85% in Hold’em, so big all-ins are less lopsided than Hold’em habits suggest. Since tournament chips lost cost more than chips won gain, skip close all-ins you do not need.
Every number here is reproducible: simulation code, workflow and replication notes on GitHub. Equities are Monte Carlo results against random opponents who never fold, with ties split: 300,000 deals per starting hand heads-up and 150,000 against five hands. The standard error of any one estimate is at most 0.09 points heads-up and 0.13 six-handed (hover a cell for its own), and a 95% margin is about twice that. Hold’em and Plus are scored on the same deals, which cuts the noise in their difference by roughly 30% but does not remove it; hover a cell for the difference and its standard error. Equity against random hands is raw strength, not expected profit against real opponents who fold, so use it to rank hands, not to size bets. All-in EV per chip staked is players × equity − 1, which assumes equal stacks all in, no rake and nobody folding. Tiers and ranges are rule-of-thumb guidance informed by these numbers, not solver output. The odds on the three-card tab are exact, from a full enumeration of flops and plus cards (sim/third_card_odds.py).
